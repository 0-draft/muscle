## Bottom line

At 172cm, 70kg, and 3–4 years of training, you still have plenty of room below the natural limit for muscle mass. Measure your **waist (at the navel)** to decide which approach to take.

| Waist | Approach | Target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Up to 84cm (abs faintly visible) | Lean bulk | Maintenance +5–10%, body weight +0.18–0.35 kg/week |
| 84–88cm | Recomp, or a short cut | Maintenance calories, protein 2.0–2.2 g/kg |
| 88cm and up | Cut | Deficit of no more than 500 kcal/day, protein 2.3–3.1 g/kg |

Every 4 weeks, review your waist and 7-day average body weight. If your waist grows 1cm or more per 1kg gained, shrink the surplus.

## Rate of gain

> [!C] When trained lifters eat a large surplus, most of the extra weight is fat. A small surplus of about **+5–15%** is enough.

- An 8-week RCT in 17 trained lifters compared maintenance, +5%, and +15%. The +15% group gained more subcutaneous fat, but muscle thickness barely differed. Rate of weight gain explained 49% of fat gain (Helms 2023)
- In a 4-week trial in 11 bodybuilders, the high-calorie group also gained much more body fat (Ribeiro 2019)
- The surplus needed to maximize hypertrophy is still unknown (Slater 2019)
- A practical target is **0.25–0.5% of body weight per week** (Iraki 2019). At 70kg, that is **0.18–0.35 kg/week**, or 0.7–1.4kg per month

If maintenance is 2,500–2,700 kcal, the surplus is **+130–400 kcal/day**. The most accurate way to find maintenance is from your log: over 2–3 weeks, find the intake where your 7-day average weight stays flat.

## Building muscle in a deficit

> [!B] A calorie deficit blunts muscle gain but not strength gain. At a deficit of about **500 kcal/day** on average, muscle gain nearly stops.

In a meta-analysis, a calorie deficit reduced lean mass gains (effect size −0.57, Murphy & Koehler 2022). If you cut, keep the deficit within 500 kcal/day (weight loss of 0.5kg per week or less).

## Recomp (changing body composition at the same body weight)

> [!C] Even trained lifters can gain muscle and lose fat at the same time with high protein and progressive training.

A review (Barakat 2020) found several RCTs in trained people that observed both at once. The exception is contest-prep athletes who are already very lean. The changes are small, though, so they are easily lost in measurement error.

## Headroom by FFMI

> [!D] At 172cm and 70kg, your FFMI is about **19–21** anywhere between 12% and 20% body fat. That leaves 4–6 points below the natural limit of **around 25**.

An FFMI (lean mass ÷ height²) of about 25 was reported as the upper limit for people not using drugs (Kouri 1995). The table below is my own calculation.

| Body fat | Lean mass | FFMI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 12% | 61.6 kg | 20.8 |
| 15% | 59.5 kg | 20.1 |
| 20% | 56.0 kg | 18.9 |

Waist-to-height ratio is a better marker of metabolic risk than BMI (Ashwell 2012). The common cutoff is 0.5, which is 86cm at 172cm tall. The boundaries in the first table are based on this.
